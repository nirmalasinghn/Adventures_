The Beginning

Shiny Rays on
Dronagiri.

It started with the shiny rays hitting Mt Dronagiri early in the morning. And then the smiles of the villagers. The mooo on the path. The puppies — sniffers, friendly, completely unbothered — following us wagging its tail. The trail was brown. There was a slight breeze, a chill that pressed gently against your face and stayed there.

And then came the alpine glow. There is a quote about mountains — that you can look at mountains for so long and still not be done. Which is exactly it. You can stare at Mt Dronagiri for so much time that it simply passes, quietly, without your permission. It stands there, strong and grounded, and the time just goes.

You can stare at mountains for so long and still not be done. Mt Dronagiri standing strong and grounded — and the time just passes when you look at mountains.
Mt Dronagiri at dawn — huge, unhurried, entirely unbothered. I could not look away.
First Night at Camp

A Bedsheet
Filled with Stars.

The night starts. The stars appear in the open sky. You might have seen the sky moving — but have you ever seen a bedsheet filled with stars moving above your head, filled with constellations, the dark sky with the brightest stars, the cold breeze freezing your body meanwhile?

Lying under the stars will always be my favourite thing to do.

02
The Trail Goes Deeper
Streams · Temples · Mules · Dogs · A forest that felt unreal
Olive green grass, mosses, pine trees standing still — it felt genuinely unreal

Woke up the next morning excited to see mountains again. I feel lucky to see that. And then the journey began to the next campsite — and on this path, so many things. The small streams of water crossing the trail. The tiny temples. The mules carrying luggage, unbothered, doing what they have always done. The dogs following us, tails wagging.

Me handing out biscuits to those dogs—by then, I was convinced this humble snack had quietly earned its place as the unofficial treat of the Himalayas.

Then the steep path — it was like a sliding in the sand competition. It was genuinely fun. And then the forest area filled with olive green grass and mosses and the tall pine trees standing there in stillness, and the breeze with it. It felt unreal. I was just walking and I found a spot — there was something about the trees there. It was fascinating.

The path next was with boulders. It's just the process of loving the path you're on — on the way to reaching the campsite. And then, the first glimpse: Hathi Parvat and Ghoda Parvat. Both of them appearing at the same time, like they had been waiting to be noticed.

Tall pine trees in stillness, olive green mosses underfoot, a breeze that felt like it had been there forever
The Campsite · Evening

180 Degrees
of Mountains.

We reached the campsite. And it was like — 180 degrees, filled with mountains. Including Hathi Parvat, Ghoda Parvat, Pangarchulla, Dronagiri, Kamet, Trishul, Nanda Devi. All of them, right there, just standing.

That evening I got to witness an epic alpine glow on all these mountains. The orange colour turning into light purple in the sky. That feeling cannot be expressed. At that time you feel free. You feel alive. You are living. You are present. It's just one of the most fascinating things to witness in your life.

The orange colour turning into light purple in the sky. That feeling cannot be expressed. At that time you feel free. You feel alive. You are living. You are present.
Alpine glow at camp — orange turning to light purple, mountains lit from within. Everything going quiet.
Night · The Temperatures Drop

Water Freezing.
The Moon. Then the Stars.

At night the water started freezing at these temperatures. The streams started freezing. The only things you were able to see in the dawn were the moon and the pine trees. Then the stars appeared later to accompany the moon — staying up and playing around in the dark, talking to strangers who very quickly stop feeling like strangers at all.

Summit Day

The Fire That
Kept Me Going.

The next morning I woke up, got ready, and just started the trek. The path was familiar now. And the fire in me — that kept me pushing, kept me going.

There was so much crowd. But the path was clear. The views were amazing. The trail was filled with grass, black ice, and an epic bridge which had frozen water — the flowing water, which was frozen. It looked like something I would have only imagined. A frozen waterfall, starting from the bridge.

The path on the ridge — wherever you go, the tiny stones and the accompanied dogs. And there we could see the peak. Filled with cairns — the piles of rocks which people worship as temples — and the flags flying high. A steady feeling. The present feeling that I am living.

On the way up I got to know why it is called Kuari Pass and not the peak. The two mountains merge together there — from that pass it leads to other peaks. That's how it gets its name. And the story that our trek lead told: Mata Parvathi, before getting married to Lord Shiva, used to come to that peak to meditate. Kuari means unmarried in Sanskrit. The mountains carry their histories quietly, and sometimes they share them with you.

And on the summit — Kamet, Nanda Devi, Pangarchulla, Hathi Parvat, Ghoda Parvat, Dronagiri, Trishul, Mt Neelkanth, Chaukhamba Massif. All of them, all at once. Right there.
The ridge to Kuari Pass — cairns, prayer flags, dogs, the whole Himalayan skyline waiting
The Way Down

Powered by Skittles
and Cookies

We finally began the descent—legs slightly questionable, energy even more so. Skittles and cookies? Absolutely, faithfully riding along in the rucksack and showing up right when morale dropped faster than our altitude. By this point, the knee had also decided to join the adventure—not dramatically, just as a constant, mildly annoying background commentary.

The mountains gave me everything I came for. And a few things I didn't even know I needed.

About the Author

Getting Lost
to Find Myself

I'm someone who believes the mountains don't ask for your résumé — they ask for your presence. This blog is my honest, unfiltered story of the trails I walk, the fears I face, and the version of myself I discover every time I lace up my boots and head up.

Kuari Pass gave me that 180-degree view I will never stop thinking about. Mountains that just stand there — Dronagiri, Nanda Devi, Kamet — entirely unbothered by everything below them. I want to be more like that.
